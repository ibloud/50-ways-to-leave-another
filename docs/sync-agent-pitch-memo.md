## What This Is

This is not a catalog submission. This is a co-production opportunity structured around an original five-song EP whose production methodology — music written before the series shoots, plot-locked to every episode — requires the music supervisor to engage at the development stage, not post.

The model is *The Nines* (John August, 2007): the main theme was composed before a frame was shot because a character performs it on screen. That was true for one scene. In this series, it is true for all five.

The EP is **50 Ways to Leave Another** by Dominique Devereaux / Loptr Lab. The title track is the series' main theme. It appears in Episode 1 as a working draft, recurs across Episodes 2–4 in alternate forms, and resolves in Episode 5 as the final recorded version. The production cannot fake this. The music must exist first.

## What Music Supervisors Need From This Project (CTRL Camp Framework)

Per CTRL Camp's published guidance on what music supervisors actually filter for — emotional tone, lyrical flexibility, edit points, and clearance readiness — here is how this project addresses each:

### Emotional tone

<cite index="21-1">A supervisor needs a song that makes you feel something specific — and the production needs to hit an emotion clearly and quickly, ideally within the first 10 to 15 seconds.</cite>

The title track's emotional function: a decision announced before the protagonist is ready to live it. The tone is not a breakup song — it is the morning after the decision, when leaving is already done and the work of reclamation has not yet started. The emotional register is: resolved on the surface, unfinished underneath. That tension is what the series runs on for five episodes.

### Lyrical flexibility

<cite index="21-1">Songs with ultra-specific narratives — names, dates, locations — are harder to place. Music supervisors gravitate toward lyrics that feel personal but could apply to a range of scenes.</cite>

The title track is written from the first person but names no one. It is about leaving without naming who was left, what they did, or where it happened. Every line is portable — it can score a scene of physical departure, emotional recurrence, or final resolution. The same lyric means something different in Episode 1 (where she has just left) and Episode 5 (where she records it finished). The music supervisor gets two distinct cue uses from one track, one song, one license.

### Edit points and arrangement

<cite index="21-1">Can the song be cut at the chorus? Does the intro work on its own under dialogue? Is there a natural build that lines up with a scene transition?</cite>

The delivery spec for each track in this EP includes: stereo master, TV mix (dialogue-safe, ducked vocal), stems, instrumental, and a cappella. The arrangement brief given to the lead artist will explicitly require modular sections — intro that works under dialogue, chorus that stands alone, a build that lines up with scene transitions. This is a production requirement built into the artist brief, not an afterthought.

### Clearance readiness

<cite index="21-1">Before a music supervisor even falls in love with your song, they need to know it can actually be used. That means clean splits, no unresolved publishing issues, and stems available if needed. No AI music. No YouTube or BeatStars beats. A great song with bad business will get passed over every time.</cite>

The commercial master will be recorded to clear all AI-origin elements from the Suno demo. The beat/instrumental license will be upgraded to SVOD/worldwide/perpetuity scope before any pitch conversation involving a specific track. The split sheet will be executed before delivery. The AI-use disclosure will accompany every deliverable.

The GitHub repo exists precisely because of this requirement: every business question a supervisor needs answered is documented before the music is finished. The repo is the clearance architecture, built in advance.

## Methodology Reference: Why Music-First Is a Plot Requirement Here

<cite index="12-1">The composer often comes on board a project while it's in post, but for *The Nines*, the main theme had to be written before shooting a frame — because during one scene, a character plays it on piano. It was a major plot point, and they couldn't fake it.</cite>

In *50 Ways to Leave Another*, this condition applies to every episode. Nova is recording the EP in real time within the story. The title track appears incomplete in Episode 1 — a working demo. It is heard in pieces across Episodes 2–4. It resolves as a finished recording in Episode 5. You cannot shoot any of these scenes without the music existing first. The music supervisor is not being brought in to score a finished picture. They are being invited into the development process because the music *is* the picture.

<cite index="12-1">When working with specialists — a composer, a cinematographer, a choreographer — August found it best to describe feelings rather than functions. He used language like: "broken clockwork," "skimming across a summer lake, but there's something dark under water," "an unanswerable question," "something half-remembered."</cite>

The creative brief for this project uses the same approach. The track-by-track creative brief (in the appendix) describes each song by emotional texture and function within the scene, not by BPM or key or production style. The music supervisor and the lead artist work from the same brief.
